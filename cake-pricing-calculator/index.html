The profit is what's left after ingredients and extras: your time, gas or electric and other costs come out of it.

How it works

  • Cost of each ingredient: the pack price divided by the pack size gives the cost per gram, millilitre or each (a 16 kg sack of flour at £14.40 is 0.09p a gram). Times what goes in the cake gives its cost. Kilograms and litres are turned into grams and millilitres for you.
  • Cost per slice: everything that goes in the cake, divided by the slices it makes, plus the extra cost per slice for boxes and boards.
  • Price to charge: the cost per slice divided by your target ingredient cost. At 30%, a slice that costs 90p to make needs a price of at least £3.00. It's rounded up to the next 5p (or whatever you set), and VAT is added when you're registered and the cake isn't zero-rated.

Why a target ingredient cost?

Ingredients are only part of what a cake costs you. The rest of the price has to cover your time, the oven, packaging you haven't counted, card fees, and leave you a profit. Bakers and cafés often aim for ingredients to be about 25% to 35% of the price. If you're selling decorated celebration cakes that take hours, your time is the biggest cost, so aim lower, or work out your hours separately and add them on.

VAT on cakes

Most small home bakers aren't registered for VAT: you only have to register once your turnover goes over £90,000 in 12 months. If you are registered, cakes are usually zero-rated, including birthday and wedding cakes, so there's no VAT to add. Cakes sold in the course of catering, such as a slice to eat in at a café, are standard-rated at 20%. Check HMRC's guidance if you're not sure which applies.
